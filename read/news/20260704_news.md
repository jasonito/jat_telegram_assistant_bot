<img src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/an-elderly-white-man-looks-at-an-exhibit-board-about-the-history-of-radio-technology.jpg?id=67080555&amp;width=1245&amp;height=700&amp;coordinates=0%2C156%2C0%2C157" /><br /><br /><p>Many IEEE members who collect historical engineering artifacts often offer them to the IEEE History and Heritage group, which includes the <a href="https://www.ieee.org/about/history-center" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE History Center</a>, to display. To bring these artifacts to the public, the group created the <a href="https://www.ieee.org/about/history-center/globalmuseum" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE Global Museum</a>, which curates traveling exhibits for display at conferences and in libraries, universities, and other venues.</p><p>The program educates people about how technological progress has unfolded over generations, and how engineers and researchers build on past achievements to benefit humanity.</p><p>Curating the exhibits has been rewarding, says <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/daniel-jon-mitchell-726b131b2" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Daniel Jon Mitchell</a>, director of the group’s heritage programs.</p><p>“People tell me that they are genuinely moved by having history and artifacts explained to them in an accessible, intelligible way,” Mitchell says. “When people are moved and emotionally affected by what you’re doing, they’re going to remember that. And I think that’s part of the power of what we’re doing.”</p><p>The most recent traveling exhibit was on display in April in New York City during the <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/ieee-celebrates-honors-ceremony-2026" target="_self">IEEE Honors Ceremony</a>, which celebrates engineering pioneers who have developed technologies that changed how people connect with the world. Attendees explored the <a href="https://history.ieee.org/programs/ieee-global-museum/microchips-that-shook-the-world/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Microchips That Shook the World</a> exhibit, which drew inspiration from <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/" target="_self"><em><em>IEEE Spectrum</em></em></a>’s <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/welcome-to-the-chip-hall-of-fame" target="_self">Chip Hall of Fame</a>. The exhibit conveys the roles integrated circuits play in fields such as signal processing, audio engineering, and telecommunications. The <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/commodore-64-cartridge" target="_self">Commodore 64</a>, one of the artifacts on display, stirred up treasured childhood memories for guests who had used the home computer.</p><p>Other exhibits have focused on early radio inventions and power and communications technologies.</p><p>The Global Museum works with IEEE societies to mark their anniversaries by interpreting and displaying pertinent items.</p><h2>A tribute to radio pioneer Edwin Howard Armstrong</h2><p>The idea of a traveling museum came to fruition in 2024 after Alexander Magoun, IEEE’s outreach historian, connected with <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/mike-molnar-2b6168b9" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Mike Molnar</a>. The IEEE associate member owns one of six <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dk6DdG4vs4Y" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">superheterodyne radio</a> prototypes developed by <a href="https://ethw.org/Technical_tour_-_Edwin_Howard_Armstrong" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Edwin Howard Armstrong, </a>who probably is best known for inventing the FM radio system. Armstrong received the first <a href="https://corporate-awards.ieee.org/ieee-medal-of-honor/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE Medal of Honor</a> in 1917.</p><p>The radio converts incoming frequencies into a fixed, lower intermediate one using a local oscillator and a frequency mixer. The technology paved the way for modern electronic communications devices. The prototype became the focal point of the Global Museum’s flagship <a href="https://www.ieeefoundation.org/the-ieee-global-museum-unveils-inaugural-exhibit-on-the-life-and-work-of-inventor-e-howard-armstrong/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Unseen Signals: E. Howard Armstrong’s Radio Revolution</a> exhibit, which celebrates the inventor’s life and his impact on the broadcasting industry and wireless communications.</p><p>“The radio prototype is one of the most incredible pieces that we could put on display,” Mitchell says. He and Magoun sourced other artifacts including an <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audion" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Audion</a> used in Armstrong’s experiments on wireless signal amplification; a selection of consumer products that attempted to cash in on radio’s popularity, including a flour sifter and laxatives; and a <a href="https://www.motorola.com/us/en/about?srsltid=AfmBOoqy3pOyL2r3X319Si_kJ1EBG-1nZAgWBf8LChcSs76T2y6W_K0U" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Motorola</a> Walkie-Talkie from the Korean War. They were from museums or private collectors along the East Coast of the United States.</p><p>“Aside from [Guglielmo] Marconi, Armstrong is the most significant contributor to the history of radio,” Mitchell says. “The exhibit is not only a biography but also a story of the cultural and political implications his work had.”</p><p>Visitors can play 15 short clips of past radio broadcasts covering politics, religion, sports, or another topic.</p><p>The Armstrong exhibit was unveiled in 2024 at the <a href="https://www.nmih.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">National Museum of Industrial History</a> in Bethlehem, Pa.</p><p>The 93-square-meter exhibit is still traveling around the United States. It is on display until 15 August at the <a href="https://pavekmuseum.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Pavek Museum</a>, in St. Louis Park, Minn.</p><p>From 21 November until 9 May 2027, it is scheduled to be at the <a href="https://misci.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Museum of Innovation and Science</a> in Schenectady, N.Y. Entry to the museum is free for IEEE members with a digital membership card.</p><h2>Collaborating with IEEE societies</h2><p>The IEEE History and Heritage group collaborates with IEEE societies to create exhibits for special events. In 2024 Mitchell curated an exhibit to celebrate the 75th anniversary of the <a href="https://vtsociety.org/home" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE Vehicular Technology Society</a> and its 100th <a href="https://events.vtsociety.org/vtc2026-spring/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Vehicular Technology Conference</a>. The Our Mobile World exhibit was launched at the conference, held in October in Washington, D.C.</p><p>“The society’s leadership helped me focus attention on key developments that meant a lot to its members,” Mitchell says.</p><p>“The IEEE Global Museum wants to present exhibits that connect with its audiences, whether these are IEEE members or the public,” he says. “Just knowing what was important historically doesn’t mean that this will resonate, so I really appreciated the insight.”</p><p>The exhibit’s artifacts included a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DynaTAC" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Motorola DynaTac</a> “brick” cellphone, a CB radio from the 1980s, and one of the earliest handheld GPS receivers. Visitors played an interactive game to test their knowledge spanning a century of wireless technology, motor vehicles, and mobile communication inventions.</p><p>Mitchell worked this year with the <a href="https://ieeedeis.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE Dielectrics and Electrical Insulation Society</a> to launch a virtual exhibit, <a href="https://history.ieee.org/powering-up/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Powering Up</a>, which is available on the Global Museum website. It provides an overview of high-voltage power engineering, and it highlights the roles that manufacturers <a href="https://www.ge.com/about-us" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">General Electric</a> and <a href="https://westinghouse.com/pages/about?srsltid=AfmBOopYcw0g8hXcfH_DYMi441eXpMmU7QrANuLS59Dm3A4N0B2aXmnC" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Westinghouse</a> played in making long-distance, high-voltage transmission of electrical power possible. Videos and photos of impulse generators and tests are featured in the exhibit.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image"> <img alt="Man in tuxedo presenting a \u201cWhat\u2019s Inside a Microchip?\u201d exhibit at an event" class="rm-shortcode" id="f4c21" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/man-in-tuxedo-presenting-a-u201cwhat-u2019s-inside-a-microchip-u201d-exhibit-at-an-event.jpg?id=67080576&amp;width=980" /> <small class="image-media media-caption">Nvidia CEO and cofounder Jensen Huang, who received the 2026 IEEE Medal of Honor, exploring the Microchips That Shook the World exhibit.</small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">IEEE Conferences, Events & Experiences</small></p><p>One photo shows lightning arcing between high-voltage generators. Others show the impulse generators used at the <a href="https://www.archives.nyc/blog/2022/4/22/the-world-of-tomorrow-1939-new-york-worlds-fair" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">1939 World’s Fair</a> in New York City, demonstrations of artificial lightning, and U.S. President <a href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/about-the-white-house/presidents/ronald-reagan/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Ronald Reagan</a> visiting GE’s high-voltage laboratory in Pittsfield, Mass.</p><h2>The history of microchips</h2><p>The Unseen Signals exhibit was created for large venues, but the Microchips That Shook the World exhibit was designed to be displayed in different spaces, Mitchell says. Artifacts are premounted to ensure easy setup, and they’re encased in glass because many are rare.</p><p>Microchips are crucial for signal processing, audio engineering, and telecommunications, making them a point of interest despite their small size, Mitchell says. One rare artifact on display is the <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/chip-hall-of-fame-kodak-kaf1300-image-sensor" target="_self">Kodak KAF-1300</a> image sensor. Invented in 1986, it was used in one of the earliest digital cameras made for photojournalists.</p><p>The KAF-1300’s image sensor chip “is credited with bringing digital cameras out of the laboratory,” Mitchell says. “Only around 500 were produced.”</p><p>Visitors can understand how transistors work, he says, by pressing buttons to turn them on and off.</p><p>“There are billions of transistors in modern microchips,” he notes, “and you can combine them in a way that performs logical functions.”</p><p>Unseen Signals, one of two identical exhibits, was curated by Mitchell and <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/u/stephen-cass" target="_self">Stephen Cass</a>, <em><em>IEEE Spectrum</em></em>’s special projects editor, with help from several <em><em>Spectrum</em></em> colleagues. Together, they served as on-site docents for guests at the IEEE Honors Ceremony.</p><p>The display also featured a preview of IEEE’s immersive “Inside the Microchip” video project, which delves beneath the silicon surface of <a href="https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Nvidia</a>’s <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/chip-hall-of-fame-nvidia-nv20" target="_self">NV20 chip</a>, using forensic photography and computer-generated renderings. The video, to be released this year, aims to teach middle school students about the microchips that are inside their gaming devices.</p><p>The exhibit was on display at the <a href="https://ectc.net/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">IEEE Electronic Components and Technology Conference</a>, held in May in Orlando, Fla. Later this year, members will be able to visit it at the <a href="https://computerhistory.org/about/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Computer History Museum</a> in Mountain View, Calif., and the <a href="https://uwaterloo.ca/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">University of Waterloo</a>, in Ontario, Canada.</p><p>The IEEE Global Museum is made possible thanks to <a href="https://secure.ieeefoundation.org/site/Donation2;jsessionid=00000000.app30124a?1400.donation=form1&amp;df_id=1400&amp;mfc_pref=T&amp;NONCE_TOKEN=C39B36563AF61D19A96420614FD1C398&amp;_gl=1*2znrqc*_ga*MjA2ODA1MTgzNC4xNzgwNjEwMzkz*_ga_65LKC85C4J*czE3ODIxNTI1MzAkbzckZzEkdDE3ODIxNTI1NjAkajMwJGwwJGgw" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">donations to the IEEE Foundation</a>.</p>
